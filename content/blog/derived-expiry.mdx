The tempting way to model an entitlement that ends — a contract, a subscription, a trial — is a
status column you flip to `expired` when the clock runs out. It reads well, it's easy to query, and
it feels like state should be stored. It's also a small trap: you've just signed up for a scheduled
job, two sources of truth, and a stretch of time where the column and reality disagree.

## The stored-status version, and why it drifts

A status you *write* needs something to write it. That something is a cron or scheduled job scanning
for newly-expired rows and flipping them. Between the moment an end date passes and the moment the
job next runs, the row says `active` and the truth says otherwise. Shorten the interval and you burn
more compute for the privilege; lengthen it and you widen the lie. Either way you now have two facts
— the dates and the status — that can disagree, and every consumer downstream has to quietly hope
the job ran on time.

## Derive it instead

Validity isn't independent state. It's a function of data you already store: the thing is active,
and *now* falls inside its window.

```sql
-- validity is computed, never stored
status = 'active' AND now() BETWEEN start_date AND end_date
```

No job, no second source of truth, no drift window. The dates are the fact; validity is computed
from them the instant you ask. Nothing to keep in sync because there's nothing stored to fall out of
sync.

## Compute it in exactly one place

Derived state has its own failure mode: five callers each re-implementing the predicate slightly
differently, one of them forgetting the `status` half, another getting the boundary wrong. So the
predicate lives *once* — a single function, or a database view or generated column — and everything
routes through it. One definition of "valid," used everywhere, is the whole point; scatter it and
you've traded a sync problem for a consistency problem.

## The tradeoffs you're accepting

- **You can't `WHERE status = 'expired'` against a stored column anymore.** If you need to filter or
  index on validity, express it as a generated column or a view over the same predicate — still one
  definition, now indexable.
- **Every read path has to go through the one predicate.** That's a discipline cost. It's also the
  same discipline that makes the guarantee hold, so it's not really extra.
- **"Now" is an input.** Tests pin it; anything auditable records the value it used. Deriving from
  the clock means being explicit about *which* clock, rather than pretending time isn't a parameter.

## Where this came from

At Lifeed, contract validity in the internal admin tool is computed exactly this way — a status plus
a date window, evaluated in one place — rather than stored as a status a job maintains. The admin
tool hands contract configuration to a non-engineering team, and "is this contract valid right now"
is precisely the kind of fact you don't want drifting between a column and the calendar while a
customer's access hangs on the answer. Store the dates, which are real; derive the verdict, which
isn't.
